Methodology

The method

Measure the instrument before you trust it, and write down what would prove you wrong before you look.

Tooling
SHA-256-sealed pre-registrations and evaluators · append-only ledgers · bootstrap CIs clustered by week, month and coin · deflated Sharpe, Hansen SPA, White's reality check
  1. Power checkWork out the smallest effect the data could detect. If the target is below it, don't run the search.
  2. Pre-registerRules, split date, bars and costs, hashed before any outcome is read.
  3. Develop in-sampleTime splits with an embargo; CIs clustered by week, month and coin; drop-the-top-5 as a gate.
  4. One-shot holdoutA lock file is written before the first label is read. One look, no retries.
  5. Attack itIndependent reviews for leakage, costs and recency, plus deflation for every trial ever run.
  6. Paper, then a gateA forward paper book behind an e-process. Promotion only writes a proposal; a person decides.
Count every trial3,038 model trials sit in one ledger. The best of them, deflated for all 3,038, has a deflated-Sharpe p of about 1.0. That is the honest answer.
Run a canaryA model trained on permuted labels scores AUC 0.47, against 0.82–0.94 on the real ones. If a canary ever passes a gate, every promotion freezes.
Count events, not tradesSample sizes are counted in independent events. 21 live Numerai rounds turn out to be about one independent observation.

A retraction, in full

ClaimedBuying a newly migrated token 1–2 seconds after the snipers had positive expected value. It replicated on a holdout and passed a grid-null test. Verdict: candidate.
CaughtAn audit of the raw data found 88 of 1,320 tick files were sparse. They skipped the trades between the pool opening and my fill, so entries were priced before the snipers had moved the price. That produced phantom winners of +400% to +4,500%. Those 88 files alone made the positive mean, the "replication" and the null pass.
CorrectedOn the 1,232 complete pools, entry loses at every delay: −5.2% a trade at 1 s (t −5.3), −3.9% at 5 s, −5.4% at 22 s (t −8.6). Verdict: RED, published the same day.
FixedThe loader now rejects sparse pools. The ML selector that had learned "few transactions means a winner" was retired, because it had learned the artefact.

Other things the process caught

  • A meme-momentum result of +1.07% a day came from corrupted third-party price data. On clean data it is −0.72% a day.
  • Shorting new perp listings made +2,666 bps an event over 18 months (t 4.69), and +295 bps (t 0.6) on the full 2023–26 tape.
  • A sealed entry model was marked PASS. The internal auditor overturned it: the deflated-Sharpe gate was missing and the baseline compared totals with totals. A "17 PASS" meta-result became 0 of 47 once every trial was counted.
  • A market maker's paper profit was mostly fiction: 60% of its paper fills had no matching volume on the real trade tape.
  • Reusing one holdout inflated measured gains 3.2×. It also hid the small negatives that would have closed a project months earlier.
  • A pre-registration file was edited after the results existed. It was disclosed, and that result was downgraded.

Research platform · 2026

Apex

The platform the second programme runs on: chain truth, a single cost model, replay, online learning and promotion gates.

Status
Paper only It never signs or sends
Scale
333 modules · 88.6k lines · 956 tests · 14 subpackages · plus ~105k lines of research code
Stack
Python · polars · LightGBM · river · solders · websockets · Parquet/zstd · launchd services

One question drives the design: can a model learn a trading policy from a live stream without fooling itself? The answer so far is "not profitably". The machinery that says so is the part worth showing.

Streaming ingest and state

Free public log streams for the pump.fun and PumpSwap programs are decoded in real time, about 215 events a second or roughly 19 million a day. Exact state is kept for the bonding curve and the constant-product pools, and decoders cover Raydium CPMM and LaunchLab and Meteora DBC and DAMM v2. A replay engine rebuilds 1,611 clean migrations from chain history to test against.

Online learners

Models score each event before learning from it, so every prediction is out of sample by construction. They include logistic regression, adaptive random forests with ADWIN drift detection, Mondrian forests and stochastic gradient trees, all with online Platt and isotonic calibration. An hourly LightGBM Q-model picks among 120 actions: three entry delays, five sizes and eight exit rules.

Promotion by e-process

A challenger is promoted only when a betting-martingale e-process (Waudby-Smith & Ramdas) on its forward per-trade returns clears max(20, K/α). K is the number of challengers in the epoch, and it never goes down, so running more models makes promotion harder. The gate also needs n ≥ 100, at least 50 distinct tokens and a positive result with the top five trades removed. Books hold one position at a time, which keeps the e-process valid when trades would overlap. Passing writes a proposal file; it never flips a switch.

The hindsight-optimal policy learner

For about 5,000 pools I priced every way each one could have been traded: 15 entry times, 7 sizes, 637 exit rules and 3 latencies, with exact AMM maths and real fees. That gives a hindsight-best action for every pool. A model then tried to predict that action from 214 features known at decision time. Split 3,016 / 999 / 1,009, holdout run once.

98.9%of training pools had a profitable action in hindsight
0.90AUC predicting which pools will be rugged
−0.020SOL a trade on the one-shot holdout (n = 674). RED

It learned which pools die, not which ones pay after fees, price impact and the rug tail. That is a clean negative, and the model now runs as a forward shadow ledger rather than being tuned until it looks better.

On-chain infrastructure · 2026

Solana execution

A streaming, pre-armed execution layer for PumpSwap pools, measured down to the microsecond, then used to prove what speed can't fix.

Status
Dry / simulation only
Stack
Python · Helius websockets (transactionSubscribe, accountSubscribe) · Anchor IDL + Borsh decoding · Helius Sender
Safety
No send path in the dry pilot (AST-checked); live send needs two independent arm switches

Newly graduated tokens move fastest in their first seconds on a pool. I wanted to know how much of a bad exit is latency and how much is the market, so I built the fast path first and measured it.

0.33msp50, trigger to ready-to-sign sell bytes (p90 1.2 ms; every tick-triggered exit ≤ 0.64 ms)
0.0bpsmedian error of my integer constant-product quote against the on-chain fill event
96.9%of 32 live migrations simulated clean at both +1 s and +2 s after detection

What is built

  • Exact pricing. The pump AMM's Anchor IDL is fetched on-chain and pinned against the published copy. Pool accounts and swap events are decoded with Borsh, and quotes use integer constant-product maths on the real reserves, fee tier included (62 of 62 tiers matched).
  • Streaming triggers. Each position subscribes to its pool's transactions from the moment it is detected. Stops, take-profits, trailing and clock exits, creator or insider sells, whale buys and rug labels are all evaluated on every transaction, not on a polling timer.
  • Pre-armed exits. The sell is assembled at entry: accounts, fee tier, tip account, compute-unit limit and a per-trigger plan. A background thread keeps the blockhash fresh. At the trigger only the amount and minimum-out are filled in.
  • Transaction builder. p50 0.035 ms with the pool cached (n = 1,000). Buys come to 1,091 bytes, well under Solana's 1,232-byte limit.
  • Fees and routing. The fee policy targets priority fees per percentile. Priced at the live market, a round trip's fees and tips fall from 0.00209 to 0.0000368 SOL. Urgent exits deliberately pay more, because landing a stop matters more than the fee.

Detection: when each feed sees a new migration, ms after the slot starts (p50)

  • PumpPortal websockethosted in New Jersey266 ms
  • Helius transaction subscribe123 ms
  • Helius migration logs98 ms

Timed against the Helius node's own first-shred timestamp for each slot (clock within ±5 ms). A Solana slot is about 254 ms at the median, so the free websocket feed arrives roughly a full slot later than the Helius feeds. Measured from London; a server beside the validators is modelled to save a further ~70 ms at the median.

The result that mattered

The dry pilot's stops were filling well past their −12% line. The obvious suspect was latency: two-second polling, and a sell built and simulated one step after another (70–130 ms). The streaming, pre-armed engine cut that path to a third of a millisecond.

Replayed on the same positions, it improved the average stop fill by 0.4 points.

Where a stop-loss actually loses, n = 16 stops, stop set at −12%

One sell jumps the pool straight through the stop, and being faster cannot recover a price that never traded. Stream pilot, three live dry runs on 24 September 2026, 63 positions. A 3,911-pool tick replay agrees: with tick-level triggers, fill minus trigger is between −0.1 and +0.7 points at one, two and three slots. Fills are simulated at the estimated landing slot, not landed.

That answer saved the next obvious spend: renting a server next to the validators. It would shave ~70 ms off each decision, and none of that reaches P&L when the loss is in the gap. The exit engine stays, because a stop that fires on the right transaction is still worth having. The money goes elsewhere.

Event shorts

Do perpetual futures fall around token unlocks and new exchange listings, after real costs?

Backtest. The unlock hold-out interval crosses zero under every clustering (week, month, coin). The per-event standard deviation of listing shorts is 17.7%, so a hold-out mean of 5.6% sits only just above its detection floor of about 5.3%.

Token unlocks

The hypothesis: insiders sell into scheduled unlocks of at least 1% of supply. The rule is to short at the open seven days before and exit two days after. Across 485 events on 44 coins it made +2.78% an event, and the effect grows with unlock size, from +1.5% for 1–2% of supply to +10.1% above 25%. The drift happens before the unlock, not after it. A 150-cell parameter grid added nothing: its best in-sample cell (+23% an event) lost 0.70% on the holdout, so only the fixed, pre-registered window counts. One data trap is worth mentioning: the exchange's candle API backfills fake pre-listing candles with zero trades, which created 48 events that never happened.

Exchange listings

The hypothesis: when a coin that already trades as a perp gets its first spot listing on a major exchange, the pump reverts. The rule is to short the day after and hold seven days. It held out of sample (t 3.24; +3.59% with the top five events removed), and it survived three independent attacks on data and leakage, execution and costs, and recency. It was still downgraded from GREEN, for three reasons: true first listings alone fail and the effect lives in later-venue listings, the pre-registration file was edited after the results, and the last 26 weeks are thin. My expectation going forward is +2–3% an event, not +5.6%.

What didn't survive

  • Buying on-chain after a listing announcement: 92% of the move happens within five seconds.
  • Trading exchange notices: 86–94% of the move is done before a one-to-two-minute entry.
  • Treasury unlocks: +4.6% in-sample, −1.0% held out. Treasury tokens mostly aren't sold.
  • Leverage on the combined book. At 1× the holdout Sharpe was 1.46, but most of it came from one crash in autumn 2025 (about +7.7% a year without it). The pre-registered 3× failed out of sample.

Research, not advice. Real costs measured since then (32–39 bps a round trip) are higher than the 23 bps the studies assumed. That is part of why the forward paper book, not the backtest, is the test.

A defended dual-momentum strategy was selected to trade live, one of fifteen. In-sample Sharpe 1.22, maximum drawdown 23.6%. The platform's drawdown matched my from-source engine exactly.

Quantiacs · £100 through the strategy, 2014–2025 Backtest

Compounded through the annual net returns, slippage included. Two years carry it, 2017 and 2021, and the rest is defence. A best-of-100 search on the same data manufactured a fake +0.30 of Sharpe against a detection floor of 0.68, which is why I stopped tuning it.

WorldQuant BRAIN

Three days stuck at zero accepted submissions. It broke when I stopped submitting single expressions and started submitting two-term combinations: correlation fell from 0.79 to 0.51 and fitness rose from 1.04 to 1.68. Gold (10,000 points) followed, with about 14 alphas live from a 416-simulation sweep. The components that made it work had each failed on their own and would have been filed as dead ends. Submissions are now paused on purpose, because each new alpha raises the correlation wall for the next.

Structural Break

Two shipped improvements gained +0.0144 on my own holdout and moved the live score by −0.0003. My holdout matched live almost exactly in level, and I used that to justify believing a delta. Levels agreeing says nothing about whether improvements transfer.

Checking someone else's numbers

A Polymarket trader turned $2,895 into $257,000 and an article explained how. I pulled 478,021 of their public on-chain events. The profit was real, +$221,120 over 97 days (t 7.52), but the mechanism everyone assumed was wrong. A second wallet in the same study looked profitable until the same filter removed 72% of its apparent profit, nearly all of it on one day. Those were redemptions of positions opened before the measurement window: an artefact I had to catch before it fooled me too.

About

I'm Charlie Wuytack. I'd rather build than consume. Most of what's on this page started as something that annoyed me: a ticket that sold out in minutes, a flight that was only cheap on paper, a laptop that throttled itself.

The trading work taught me my most useful habit: decide what would prove me wrong before I look, then go looking for it. Most of what I've tested didn't survive that, and I'd rather show you the kill log than a backtest that flatters me.

Happy to walk through any of it in more depth: the code, the data, or the failures.
